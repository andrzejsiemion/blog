Every car owner sooner or later asks themselves the same question: how much does this thing actually cost me per month? Fuel is the obvious part, but then there's insurance, tyres, service, the inspection sticker, that one weird noise that turned out to be a wheel bearing. And the moment you have more than one car in the household, keeping all of that in a spreadsheet starts to feel less like a hobby and more like a part-time job.

The market is full of apps that promise to help. Most of them follow the same script: free download, a couple of vehicles allowed for free, then a subscription to unlock the rest. Cloud sync — to *their* cloud, of course, with an account, an email confirmation, and a privacy policy nobody reads. Ads between fill-ups. Statistics behind another paywall. The data of your own car, locked away on someone else's server, available "as a service" for as long as the company decides to keep the lights on.

I wanted something different: an app that just tracks my fuel and costs, works offline, doesn't ask for an account, and doesn't try to monetize the fact that I drive a car. So I built one — with Claude as the co-pilot — and called it [Drivest](https://apps.apple.com/app/drivest-app/id6765852658). It's free, open-source, and everything stays on the device.

Simple and functional: add a vehicle, log fill-ups, log costs, see how much you actually spend. No account, no cloud, no ads, no subscription tiers. If you want to move your data to another phone, you export it to a file and import it on the other side.

Under the hood Drivest is SwiftUI + SwiftData on iOS 17, MVVM, Apple frameworks only — no third-party packages. Each fill-up captures price, volume, odometer and (optionally) a GPS location, and the app derives efficiency from there: per-tank, period, and even a daily figure when the connected car API gives you mileage often enough. Costs are categorized (insurance, service, tyres and so on) and you can set reminders that fire either on a date or after a certain distance — the kind of thing that decides whether you remember the inspection sticker before or after the fine. Electric cars get first-class treatment: electricity bills, energy snapshots, and an EV-aware efficiency view. Multi-currency is handled with live NBP exchange rates, so a fill-up abroad in EUR sits cleanly next to PLN entries in the statistics. For Volvo and Toyota owners there's an optional integration with the manufacturer APIs that pulls the odometer in the background — credentials live in the iOS Keychain, never leave the device, and the rest of the app works exactly the same without them. Fill-ups can be plotted on a map, clustered when zoomed out, and there's a receipt scanner that pre-fills the form from a photo of the slip. Export/import is a versioned format that round-trips photos and location data. The app is bilingual (English and Polish) and supports dark mode end to end.

What's cool about it? Same story as Scandio — I had never written Swift before Drivest, and I built it in the open with spec-driven development. Each feature lives in its own folder under `specs/` with a plan written first, the code generated against that plan, and the spec updated when the design changes. By now there are over forty of those folders — fuel tracking, vehicle photos, cost categories, statistics filters, dual currency, EV charging, export/import, connected odometer services, reminders, GPS, maps, clustering, daily efficiency from the API — each one a self-contained loop of "describe what I want, let Claude build it, review, adjust." It scales surprisingly well: features added a year apart still read like the same app.

Would Drivest exist without Claude? Honestly, no. I would have given up before finishing the second screen. The fact that I can now open my phone at the petrol station, log a fill-up in ten seconds, and a week later see a clean chart of how much my cars cost me this quarter — that whole thing is a side effect of being able to describe software instead of having to hand-write it.

The code is [on GitHub](https://github.com/andrzejsiemion/drivest-ios) if anyone wants to read it, fork it, or port it somewhere else.
